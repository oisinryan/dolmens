Standing waves, Helmholtz pressure vessels, and six Neolithic & Iron Age stone chambers

In 1994, engineers Robert Jahn, Paul Devereux, and Michael Ibison measured six prehistoric stone chambers across Britain and Ireland and found every one resonated tightly between 95 and 120 Hz — squarely inside the adult male voice range.     Sections 08 – 10 & Sources

    The 110-Hz Brain, Cave Acoustics & Weighing the Case

    UCLA EEG Pilot (Cook, Pajot & Leuchter 2008) & Cave Evidence

    • Cook et al. (2008): 30 adults exposed to 90, 100, 110, 120 & 130 Hz showed reduced left-temporal (language) activity and frontal shift at 110 Hz.
    • Watson & Was (2021) Caveat: Noted “no robust scientific evidence that single frequencies can trigger specific physiological effects” and flagged inconsistencies in the 2008 pilot.
    • Palaeolithic Caves (Fazenda et al. 2017): Across 5 northern Spanish caves (up to 40,000 yrs old), lines/dots showed a “statistically weak but tenable” link to low-frequency rock resonances.
    • Etruscan Tombs (Ortoleva & Forte 2026): 5th-century BCE painted tombs exhibited engineered reverberation built to affect living ritual listeners.

    Mainstream Accidental Case vs Learned-by-Ear Craft

    Mainstream / Accidental Physics Case

    Build any dry-stone room to human scale (~1.5–4.7 m) and low-frequency standing waves inevitably land in the 95–120 Hz male voice range. Only one pilot season (1994) mapped 6 sites, Newgrange has zero Helmholtz pulse, and Jahn, Watson & Was all state builder intent cannot be proved.

    Learned-by-Ear Architectural Craft

    What stays striking is accumulation: Europe’s finest Neolithic stonework at Maeshowe is square (stacking 109.8 Hz), clay-sealed (holding 2 Hz at 120 dB), and coupled past a recessed blocking stone; Loughcrew’s partitions shift resonance cleanly to 108–110 Hz; and Newgrange’s solstice slot is also an effective acoustic aperture into its 19 m pipe.
